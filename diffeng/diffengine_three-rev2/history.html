Lynn Hershman conceptualized the Difference Engine #3 in 1995, using Charles Babbage's original Difference Engine (commonly considered the world's first computer) as a reference. The original machine was used to calculate numerical positions, called polynomials, via the interaction of its component parts. After mechanically entering values for the initial x, the initial y, the x-step, and the nine coefficients of the polynomial, the machine was then hand-cranked, yielding calculations that proved useful in navigation, science, surveying and finances.

Hershman and her collaborators found a correlation between the dynamics of this machine and the conceptual environment of 3-Dimensional Cyberspace: both work from a set of numerical positions that are poised to influence each other. If the coordinates of a virtual world could be substituted for the initial x , and a digitally captured and numbered representation of museum visitors for the initial y, perhaps the calculus of this new machine might yield an exploration of the ethical and physical boundaries of virtual space - boundaries that intersect with revised notions of identity, voyeurism, surveillance, and the digital absorption and spiritual transformation of the physical body.
