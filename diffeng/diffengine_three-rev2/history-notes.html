How It Works

The installation will consist of three stations called Bi-directional Browsing Units (BBUs). On each BBU there is a three dimensional floor plan or "view" which corresponds to the BBU's physical location within the museum. By grabbing the BBU and rotating or tilting it, the museum visitor causes shifting perspectives within the virtual world.

At the same time, as one approaches the unit, light sensors trigger digital cameras attached to the BBUs, causing them to snap around and capture the visitor's image. This image - generally the face of the museum visitor -is then mapped onto a torso-less Avatar; the eyes are displaced and replaced with identification numbers.

In its dormant position, the digital camera faces out into the museum and sends a live video signal back into cyberspace, affording visitors from all over the world a view into a museum to which they might otherwise not have access. Through their browser interface, the virtual visitors can determine the perspective of their view into the museum by sending commands to the BBUs, causing it to tilt and rotate much as the "real" visitor would by grabbing it.

Occassionally a person touching the BBU in the museum will "feel" an internet person trying to move it at the same time. Or, alternately, the BBU will appear to move on its own. In this way, the BBUs act as a mirror link between "real" visitors to the museum and those who lurk "virtually" on the internet, reflecting traces of presence 1.) from the internet into the physical space of the ZKM Museum and 2.) from the halls of the Museum out into cyberspace.

The virtual visitors, like the "real" visitors, also have Avatars created in their image at the moment of their first encounter with the multi-user environment. While the internet visitors determine the path their avatar-presence takes through the virtual museum, the "real" visitor's avatar travels a pre-determined path in a gesture that subverts notions of control over self-representation.

The life expectancy of each avatar is one hour, after which they slip through a hole in cyberspace and suspend in Purgatory until they are displaced by more recent visitors. This middle leg of the avatars' journey can be viewed at the entrance to the museum, where a large LED Plasma Screen displays the rotating, three dimensional Purgatory space.

Life after Purgatory equals Archive. Finally, each avatar must be resigned to a less active state. The Archive houses 20,000 avatars according to the number they were assigned at birth. These identification numbers correspond to the number of visitors the piece has logged. This number can later be used to call the Avatar up from its final resting place at any point in time.
